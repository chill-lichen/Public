Sample work

Here's what a monthly owner report could look like.

Imagine a small service firm that invoices clients, tracks project costs, and often waits weeks to be paid. The sample below brings a few of those numbers together. Pick a month and see what's changed.

Every number on this page is fictional. This is a demonstration, not a client case study or an accounting product.

What I'd ask next

Look at the collections gap.

In Jun, invoices were $4,400 higher than payments received. Customers owed $24,100 at month-end; $11,900 (49%) was overdue. That's a collection question worth examining before adding expenses.

Invoiced amount less estimated direct delivery costs:

$19,500

45.8% of invoiced work, before overhead

This is not net profit or cash flow. It excludes rent, general overhead, interest, taxes and owner compensation unless included in direct delivery costs.

One more useful question: which service contributes most?

This example is for June only. Estimated direct delivery costs are assigned to each service, but general overhead is not. Contribution is not net profit.

How the sample holds together: The firm started January with $8,600 unpaid. Each month, closing unpaid invoices equal the prior month's balance plus invoices sent, minus customer payments received. Overdue invoices are a subset of unpaid invoices. Delivery costs are estimates; this is not bookkeeping or an audited financial statement.
What the real project involves

The chart is the last step.

First we'd check what your accounting or invoicing tool actually records, where the hours and costs come from, and whether the definitions make sense. Then we'd build a report using the information available. If a metric can't be calculated reliably, I'd say so.

The important deliverable is a repeatable way to produce the right answers—not a pretty screenshot.
